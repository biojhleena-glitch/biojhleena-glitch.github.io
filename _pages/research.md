My research connects molecular design, therapeutic delivery, and disease biology. I develop **DNA-inspired Janus base nanomaterials**—structures assembled from small molecular building blocks—to investigate two linked challenges: reaching cells within difficult-to-penetrate tissues and releasing therapeutic cargo inside those cells.

My long-term goal is to develop targeted RNA nanomedicines for inflammatory and degenerative diseases. Published studies in intracellular delivery and cancer provide a foundation for my current osteoarthritis research and my proposed work in immune-targeted RNA delivery.

Getting RNA into a cell is only part of the challenge. It must also escape the endosome, a membrane-bound compartment that can trap the cargo. I investigate how Janus base nanopieces package RNA and support its release into the cytoplasm, where it can act.

In my first-author [PNAS study (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2104511118), I developed and evaluated DNA-inspired carriers for siRNA delivery, including uptake, endosomal escape, and functional gene silencing. This work informs how I design and test new RNA formulations.

More recently, I contributed as co-corresponding author to our [Materials Today Advances study (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mtadv.2026.100845), which investigated nanoparticle-enabled co-delivery of doxorubicin and siRNA to address multidrug resistance in preclinical cancer models.

## Nanoparticle design & tissue transport

<p class="research-status">Published research</p>

A carrier’s molecular composition, assembly, and geometry can influence its path through tissue. I combine computation-informed formulation with experimental characterization to study these relationships, including the behavior of rod-shaped Janus base nanoparticles.

As co-first author of our [Journal of Controlled Release study (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jconrel.2025.114169), I helped design and evaluate rod-shaped carriers for drug loading, penetration into three-dimensional tumor spheroids, and delivery in an ovarian cancer xenograft model. The findings motivate further study of how material properties influence delivery across different biological environments.

<p class="research-keywords">Molecular assembly / rod-shaped carriers / tumor penetration / structure–function relationships</p>

## RNA therapeutics & engineered tissue models

<p class="research-status">Current research · Osteoarthritis</p>

I am developing Janus base nanopieces for **Atsttrin mRNA delivery** alongside a **human cartilage-on-a-chip model**. The aim is to connect therapeutic formulation with measurements of inflammation, cartilage degradation, and tissue response.

This work builds on my doctoral studies of local mRNA delivery to joint tissues. Current development includes a fabricated tissue-chip prototype and preliminary molecular-response studies; therapeutic performance remains under investigation.

I am the principal investigator of the [2026–2027 ORS/Bethel Fellowship project](https://www.bethel.pitt.edu/2026/09/03/advancing-discovery-bmrc-welcomes-its-newest-cohort-of-bethel-fellows/) supporting this direction, with Yupeng Chen and Chuan-Ju Liu as co-investigators. The awarded fellowship begins in October 2026.

<p class="research-status">Proposed research program</p>
<h2 id="vision-title">Future research vision</h2>

I envision a program that builds from targeted RNA delivery in joint disease toward delivery to inflammatory sites in the central nervous system (CNS). Each direction will require its own validation of targeting, distribution, biological activity, and tolerability.

### Targeted RNA therapy for rheumatoid arthritis

I plan to engineer carriers that reach inflamed synovial tissue and deliver RNA to activated macrophages. A central proposed approach combines folate-modified rod-shaped particles with circular RNA encoding Atsttrin. The goal is to test whether formulation and cell targeting can improve local delivery and the duration of anti-inflammatory activity.

### RNA delivery for CNS vascular inflammation

My proposed next step is to investigate delivery through the cerebrospinal fluid to sites of vascular inflammation, including CNS vasculitis. Early studies would establish where the particles travel, which cells they reach, and whether candidate targeting receptors are present in the relevant disease models. Transport and targeting in the CNS remain open research questions.

### Longer-term gene regulation and editing

Building on RNA delivery studies, I aim to explore nonviral delivery of CRISPR-based tools to regulate inflammatory pathways. This longer-term direction would depend on establishing effective delivery and disease-relevant targets, with careful evaluation of specificity, durability, and tolerability.
